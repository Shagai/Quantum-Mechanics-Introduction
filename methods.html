Approximation methods connect the formalism to real systems.

This page covers perturbation theory, degenerate mixing, Stark splitting, variational estimates, WKB, scattering amplitudes, cross sections, and time-dependent transitions.

Methods 1. Beyond exact solutions

Most real problems are solved by controlled approximation.

Once the formalism is in place, the work becomes strategic: identify a small parameter, a good trial space, a symmetry, or a regime where phases simplify. Approximation methods are not shortcuts around quantum mechanics; they are how the theory becomes useful.

\[ \hat H=\hat H_0+\lambda x^4,\qquad E_n^{(0)}=n+\frac12\quad(\hbar=m=\omega=1) \] \[ E_n\approx E_n^{(0)}+\lambda\langle n^{(0)}|x^4|n^{(0)}\rangle =n+\frac12+\frac{3\lambda}{4}(2n^2+2n+1). \] \[ |n\rangle\approx |n^{(0)}\rangle+ \lambda\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{\langle m^{(0)}|x^4|n^{(0)}\rangle} {E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}|m^{(0)}\rangle. \]
\[ W_{ij}=\langle i^{(0)}|\hat H'|j^{(0)}\rangle,\qquad i,j\in\mathcal D. \] \[ \det(W-wI)=0,\qquad E_\alpha\approx E^{(0)}+\lambda w_\alpha. \] \[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}= \begin{pmatrix} -\Delta/2 & V\\ V & \Delta/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{(\Delta/2)^2+V^2}. \]
\[ \hat H'=-qEz,\qquad \langle n\ell m|z|n\ell m\rangle=0 \quad \text{for a nondegenerate parity eigenstate.} \] \[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}= \begin{pmatrix} -\Delta/2 & -dE\\ -dE & \Delta/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{(\Delta/2)^2+d^2E^2}. \] \[ \Delta=0 \Rightarrow E_\pm=\pm |dE| \quad\text{linear Stark effect},\qquad |dE|\ll\Delta \Rightarrow \Delta E\simeq-\frac{d^2E^2}{\Delta}. \]

Degeneracy changes the power of the field: linear splitting signals that the perturbation mixes states before ordinary second-order formulas are valid.

\[ \phi_\alpha(x)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha x^2/2} \] \[ E(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{4}+\frac{1}{4\alpha}+\frac{3\lambda}{4\alpha^2}, \qquad E_0\le E(\alpha). \]
\[ p(x)=\sqrt{2m(E-V(x))},\qquad \psi_{\text{WKB}}(x)\approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{p(x)}} \sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_1}^{x}p(x')\,dx'+\frac{\pi}{4}\right). \] \[ \int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)\,dx=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi\hbar. \] \[ \psi(x)\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}} \exp\left[-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_2}^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx'\right], \qquad \kappa(x)=\sqrt{2m(V(x)-E)}. \]

Perturbation theory

If \(\hat H=\hat H_0+\lambda\hat H'\), then the first-order energy correction is \[ E_n^{(1)}=\langle n^{(0)}|\hat H'|n^{(0)}\rangle. \] The method is strongest when nearby unperturbed levels are well separated.

Variational principle

Any normalized trial state gives an upper bound on the ground energy: \[ E_0\le \langle\phi|\hat H|\phi\rangle. \] Good physics enters through a trial family with meaningful parameters.

WKB

In slowly varying potentials, \[ \psi(x)\approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\exp\left(\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^x p(x')\,dx'\right). \] Turning points require connection formulas.

Scattering

Far from a localized potential, \[ \psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r} \] \[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2. \]

Spin and angular momentum

The algebra \[ [\hat J_i,\hat J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\hat J_k \] determines ladder operators, magnetic quantum numbers, and selection rules.

Identical particles

Exchange symmetry imposes \[ \Psi(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2)=\pm\Psi(\mathbf r_2,\mathbf r_1). \] The minus sign for fermions leads to the Pauli exclusion principle.

Problem ladder for deep study

  1. Normalize a Gaussian wave packet and compute \(\langle x\rangle\), \(\langle p\rangle\), \(\Delta x\), and \(\Delta p\).
  2. Solve the infinite square well and prove orthogonality of \(\psi_m\) and \(\psi_n\).
  3. Use ladder operators to derive the harmonic oscillator spectrum without solving a differential equation.
  4. Compute the first-order Stark shift in a simple two-level truncation.
  5. Use WKB quantization to estimate bound-state energies in a slowly varying potential.
  6. Derive a differential cross section from a given scattering amplitude.

Methods 2. Scattering

Scattering turns a potential into an angular probability map.

In a scattering experiment, a beam comes in with a well-defined momentum and the detector records how much probability is redirected into each solid angle. Far from the target, the wavefunction has an incoming plane-wave part and an outgoing spherical-wave part.

The measurable object is the differential cross section: \(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2\). In the Born approximation, \(f(\theta)\) is controlled by the Fourier transform of the potential at momentum transfer \(q=2k\sin(\theta/2)\).

Asymptotic form

\[ \psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}, \qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2. \]

Momentum transfer

\[ q=|\mathbf k'-\mathbf k|=2k\sin\frac{\theta}{2}. \] Small angles mean small momentum transfer; backward scattering probes shorter structure.

Born approximation

\[ f(\theta)\approx-\frac{2m}{4\pi\hbar^2} \int e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}V(\mathbf r)\,d^3r. \] The first Born approximation is strongest when the potential is weak enough that one scattering event dominates.

\[ V(r)=\frac{\kappa}{r},\qquad \eta=\frac{m\kappa}{\hbar^2 k}. \] \[ f_C(\theta)=-\frac{\eta}{2k\sin^2(\theta/2)} \exp\left[-2i\eta\ln\sin\frac{\theta}{2}+2i\sigma_0\right]. \] \[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} =\frac{\eta^2}{4k^2\sin^4(\theta/2)} =\left(\frac{\kappa}{4E}\right)^2\csc^4\frac{\theta}{2}. \]

The Coulomb phase changes the amplitude, but the measurable angular distribution matches the classical Rutherford law.

\[ f(\theta)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_\ell(\theta), \] \[ a_\ell(\theta)=(2\ell+1)e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta). \] \[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2. \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{4\pi}{k^2} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\sin^2\delta_\ell. \]

\(\ell=0\) gives isotropic \(s\)-wave scattering; higher \(\ell\) add angular structure.

\[ f(\theta)=\frac{1}{2ik}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1) \left(S_\ell-1\right)P_\ell(\cos\theta), \qquad S_\ell=\eta_\ell e^{2i\delta_\ell}. \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{4\pi}{k}\operatorname{Im} f(0). \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)|1-S_\ell|^2, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{abs}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)(1-\eta_\ell^2). \]

With \(\eta_\ell=1\), scattering is purely elastic; \(\eta_\ell<1\) means probability is removed into other channels, but the optical theorem still balances the flux.

Methods 3. Time-dependent transitions

A time-dependent perturbation moves probability between levels.

Stationary states do not change their measurement probabilities by themselves. Transitions happen when the Hamiltonian contains a time-dependent perturbation, such as an oscillating electric field coupling two atomic levels.

For a driven two-level system, resonance matters. If the drive frequency is close to the level spacing, probability can slosh coherently between \(|g\rangle\) and \(|e\rangle\). With detuning \(\Delta\) and coupling \(\Omega\), the excited-state probability follows the Rabi form.

Driven two-level probability

\[ P_e(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega_R^2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\Omega_R t}{2}\right), \qquad \Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}. \]

Transition amplitude

First-order time-dependent perturbation theory gives \[ c_f^{(1)}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t \langle f|\hat H'(t')|i\rangle e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt'. \] Resonant phases add coherently; off-resonant phases mostly cancel.

Fermi golden rule

For transitions into many nearby final states, \[ \Gamma_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar} |\langle f|\hat H'|i\rangle|^2\rho(E_f). \] The density of final states turns coherent amplitude growth into a transition rate.

\[ \langle n'\ell'm'|\hat{\mathbf d}\cdot\mathbf e_q|n\ell m\rangle \propto R_{n'\ell',n\ell}\int Y_{\ell'}^{m'*}Y_1^qY_\ell^m\,d\Omega. \] \[ \Delta \ell=\pm1,\qquad \Delta m=q,\qquad q=0,\pm1. \] \[ \Pi_f\Pi_i=-1 \quad \text{because } \hat{\mathbf d}=-e\mathbf r \text{ is odd under parity.} \]

A transition can be resonant and still dark if the angular integral vanishes by symmetry.

\[ E_{n,j}\simeq -\frac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^2} \left[ 1+\frac{\alpha^2}{n^2} \left(\frac{n}{j+\tfrac12}-\frac34\right) \right]. \] \[ j=\ell\pm\tfrac12,\qquad m_j=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j. \] \[ g_j=1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-\ell(\ell+1)}{2j(j+1)},\qquad \Delta E_Z=\mu_B g_j m_j B. \]

Fine structure depends on \(j\), while the weak-field Zeeman shift resolves the \(m_j\) degeneracy.

\[ \hat H_{\mathrm{hf}}=A\,\hat{\mathbf I}\cdot\hat{\mathbf J} =\frac{A}{2}\left(\hat F^2-\hat I^2-\hat J^2\right), \qquad \hat{\mathbf F}=\hat{\mathbf I}+\hat{\mathbf J}. \] \[ F=|I-J|, |I-J|+1,\ldots,I+J, \qquad E_F=\frac{A}{2}\left[F(F+1)-I(I+1)-J(J+1)\right]. \] \[ \Delta E_{F,m_F}\simeq g_F\mu_B m_F B, \qquad m_F=-F,-F+1,\ldots,F. \]

Hyperfine spectroscopy is a precision probe: the ground-state hydrogen \(F=1\to F=0\) splitting is the radio line used to map neutral hydrogen in astronomy.

\[ |\psi(0)\rangle=|n(0)\rangle \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |\psi(t)\rangle\approx e^{i\theta_n(t)}e^{i\gamma_n(t)}|n(t)\rangle. \] \[ \left|\frac{\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle}{(E_m-E_n)^2}\right|\ll\frac{1}{\hbar} \quad (m\ne n). \] \[ \gamma_n=i\int \langle n(R)|\nabla_R n(R)\rangle\cdot dR. \] \[ \gamma_\pm=\mp\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{solid}}}{2} \quad \text{for spin-\(\tfrac12\) around a closed cone.} \]

The dynamical phase depends on elapsed time and energy; the Berry phase depends only on the path in parameter space.
