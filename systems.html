Systems 1. Solvable systems

Exact solutions teach the grammar of spectra.

The time-independent Schrodinger equation is an eigenvalue problem: \( \hat H\psi_n = E_n\psi_n \). Bound states are normalizable eigenfunctions. Their energy spectrum can be discrete, continuous, or mixed depending on the potential and boundary conditions.

\[ \int_0^a \psi_n^*(x)\psi_m(x)\,dx=\delta_{nm},\qquad \Psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}. \] \[ c_n=\int_0^a \psi_n^*(x)\Psi(x,0)\,dx,\qquad \sum_n |c_n|^2=1. \]

Orthogonality is the algebra behind spectral expansion: projecting onto one eigenfunction removes every other stationary component.

\[ \Psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)e^{i(kx-\omega(k)t)}\,dk,\qquad \omega(k)=\frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}. \] \[ \langle x\rangle=x_0+\frac{\hbar k_0}{m}t,\qquad \sigma_x(t)=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0^2}\right)^2}. \]

The center follows the classical free-particle law, while the width grows because the dispersion relation is quadratic. This is the dynamical side of the Fourier uncertainty relation.

\[ z=\frac{ka}{2},\qquad z_0=\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}},\qquad \kappa a/2=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}. \] \[ \text{even: } z\tan z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2},\qquad \text{odd: } -z\cot z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}. \]

Finite walls make the boundary condition softer: \(\psi\) and \(d\psi/dx\) remain continuous, but the tail outside the well is exponential. As \(V_0\) grows, the roots move toward the infinite-well spectrum.

\[ (\hat P\psi)(x)=\psi(-x),\qquad \hat P^2=I,\qquad [\hat H,\hat P]=0 \quad \text{if } V(x)=V(-x). \] \[ \hat P|e\rangle=+|e\rangle,\qquad \hat P|o\rangle=-|o\rangle,\qquad \langle e|\hat x|e\rangle=\langle o|\hat x|o\rangle=0. \] \[ \langle e|\hat x|o\rangle \text{ can be nonzero, so odd perturbations mix opposite parity.} \]

Parity is a shortcut for calculation: many integrals vanish before you compute them because the integrand is odd.

\[ V(x)=-\alpha\delta(x),\qquad \psi_b(x)=\sqrt{\kappa}\,e^{-\kappa |x|},\qquad E_b=-\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m},\quad \kappa=\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2}. \] \[ \psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-) =-\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\psi(0),\qquad T=\frac{k^2}{k^2+\kappa^2},\quad R=\frac{\kappa^2}{k^2+\kappa^2}. \]

The wavefunction remains continuous, but its slope jumps at the spike. This is the cleanest place to see how boundary conditions can encode a singular interaction.

\[ \Psi(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}, \qquad c_n=\int_0^a\psi_n^*(x)\Psi(x,0)\,dx. \] \[ P(E_n)=|c_n|^2,\qquad \langle H\rangle=\sum_n |c_n|^2E_n. \]

An energy eigenstate is stationary because only an overall phase changes. A superposition is different: phase differences \(e^{-i(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar}\) move probability around the well.

Infinite square well

\[ \psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}, \qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}. \]

Harmonic oscillator

\[ \hat H=\hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\right), \qquad E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right). \]

Tunneling

For a barrier with \(E\lt V_0\), the wave decays inside the classically forbidden region: \[ \psi(x)\sim e^{-\kappa x}, \qquad \kappa=\sqrt{\frac{2m(V_0-E)}{\hbar^2}}. \]

Systems 2. Harmonic oscillator

The harmonic oscillator is the algebraic engine of quantum mechanics.

Near a stable equilibrium, many potentials look quadratic: \(V(x)\approx \frac12m\omega^2x^2\). The oscillator is therefore more than a toy model; it is the local language of vibrations, fields, photons, and phonons.

Its Hamiltonian can be factored into ladder operators. Instead of solving a new differential equation for each level, one climbs the spectrum: \(\hat a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle\).

Hamiltonian and ladder operators

\[ \hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2, \qquad \hat H=\hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\right). \]

Number states

\[ \hat N|n\rangle=n|n\rangle,\qquad E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots \] The zero-point energy is forced by the commutator \([\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1\).

Position-space wavefunctions

With \(\xi=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x\), \[ \psi_n(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n n!}}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4} H_n(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/2}. \]

Systems 3. Three dimensions

Hydrogen turns symmetry into quantum numbers.

For any central potential \(V(r)\), the Schrodinger equation separates into radial and angular parts. In the Coulomb potential, the energy depends only on the principal quantum number \(n\), while \(\ell\) and \(m\) describe angular momentum and orientation.

The wavefunction has the form \(\Psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)\). The radial probability density is \(P(r)=r^2|R_{n\ell}(r)|^2\).

Central-potential separation

\[ \Psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi), \qquad \hat L^2Y_\ell^m=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_\ell^m. \]

Hydrogen spectrum

\[ E_n=-\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots \] The degeneracy reflects the Coulomb potential's extra symmetry.

\[ \hat L^2Y_\ell^m=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_\ell^m,\qquad \hat L_zY_\ell^m=\hbar mY_\ell^m. \] \[ Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)=N_{\ell m}P_\ell^{|m|}(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},\qquad m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell. \]

The \(\theta\)-dependence controls polar nodes, while \(e^{im\phi}\) winds the phase around the \(z\)-axis. These are the angular pieces that later control selection rules and partial waves.
