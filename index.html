An interactive first pass

Quantum physics starts when probability gets a phase.

Classical probability tells us how often outcomes happen. Quantum mechanics adds a hidden bookkeeping layer: amplitudes can point in different directions before becoming probabilities.

1. The state

Quantum states are not ordinary probability lists.

A quantum state stores amplitudes. An amplitude has a size and a phase. When a measurement is made, the size becomes an ordinary probability through the Born rule: \(P(\text{outcome})=|\text{amplitude}|^2\).

The phase is invisible if there is only one path. It matters when two or more paths can meet. Then amplitudes add before we square them, so phases can strengthen or cancel each other.

\[ \phi(p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)e^{-ipx/\hbar}\,dx, \qquad \psi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(p)e^{ipx/\hbar}\,dp. \] \[ P(x)\,dx=|\psi(x)|^2dx,\qquad P(p)\,dp=|\phi(p)|^2dp,\qquad \langle p\rangle=\int \phi^*(p)p\phi(p)\,dp. \]

The uncertainty relation is a Fourier fact with physical consequences: a narrow packet needs many momentum components, while a nearly single-momentum state must spread across space.

\[ \hat p=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\qquad \hat T(a)=e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar},\qquad (\hat T(a)\psi)(x)=\psi(x-a). \] \[ [\hat x,\hat p]\psi =\hat x(-i\hbar\partial_x\psi) +i\hbar\partial_x(x\psi) =i\hbar\psi. \]

This is why the canonical commutator is not arbitrary notation: it encodes how positions change under infinitesimal translations.

2. The experiment

The double slit is phase bookkeeping made visible.

If a particle can reach a detector through two indistinguishable paths, the amplitudes from both paths are added first. Where phases line up, detection is likely. Where they oppose, detection is unlikely.

This is why the pattern is not the sum of two classical shadows. It is a probability distribution created by amplitude addition.

3. The smallest example

A qubit is a point of direction, not a coin waiting to land.

A classical bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit can be in a superposition \(|\psi\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle\). The angle theta controls the measurement probabilities in the 0 and 1 basis. The angle phi is a relative phase that becomes visible in other measurement bases.

4. The update

Measurement turns amplitude into sampled fact.

Quantum mechanics predicts probabilities for outcomes, not a hidden list of prewritten answers. Repeating the same preparation many times reveals the probability distribution.

\[ \hat P_a=\sum_{\mu}|a,\mu\rangle\langle a,\mu|,\qquad p(a)=\langle\psi|\hat P_a|\psi\rangle. \] \[ |\psi\rangle\longrightarrow \frac{\hat P_a|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi|\hat P_a|\psi\rangle}}, \qquad \hat P_a\hat P_b=\delta_{ab}\hat P_a,\quad \sum_a\hat P_a=I. \]

The probability is the length squared of the projected component. For a degenerate eigenspace, the relative amplitudes inside that subspace survive the measurement.

5. The constraint

Uncertainty is a limit on jointly sharp descriptions.

A localized wave packet is built from many momentum components. A broad packet can be made from a narrower range of momenta. That tradeoff is the content of the uncertainty principle: \( \Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2 \).

\[ \Delta A=\sqrt{\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2},\qquad \Delta B=\sqrt{\langle B^2\rangle-\langle B\rangle^2}. \] \[ \Delta A\,\Delta B\geq \frac{1}{2}\left|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\right|. \] \[ A=\sigma_z,\qquad B(\alpha)=\cos\alpha\,\sigma_z+\sin\alpha\,\sigma_x,\qquad \frac{1}{2}|\langle[A,B]\rangle|=|\sin\alpha\,\langle\sigma_y\rangle|. \]

The inequality is a statement about a prepared state, not about bad instruments. It comes from Cauchy-Schwarz applied to the vectors \((A-\langle A\rangle)|\psi\rangle\) and \((B-\langle B\rangle)|\psi\rangle\).

\[ \rho(x,t)=|\Psi(x,t)|^2,\qquad j(x,t)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\!\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right). \] \[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}=0,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\int_a^b \rho(x,t)\,dx=j(a,t)-j(b,t). \]

This is the local version of normalization. The Schrödinger equation does not merely preserve total probability; it says where probability flows.

7. The postulates

The rules are compact; the consequences are large.

A pure state is a normalized vector \(|\psi\rangle\) in a complex Hilbert space. Observables are represented by Hermitian operators. If \(\hat A|a_n\rangle=a_n|a_n\rangle\), then measuring \(A\) returns one of the eigenvalues \(a_n\), with probability \(|\langle a_n|\psi\rangle|^2\).

Time evolution is generated by the Hamiltonian. For a time-independent Hamiltonian, the formal solution is unitary: \( |\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle \).

Normalization

Probability one means \[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx = 1. \] If \(\Psi\) is multiplied by a constant, normalization fixes the allowed constant.

Expectation values

The average result over repeated preparations is \[ \langle A\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle. \] In position space, \(\langle x\rangle=\int x|\Psi(x,t)|^2dx\).

Compatible measurements

Operators commute when there is a common eigenbasis: \[ [\hat A,\hat B]=0. \] Nonzero commutators are the algebraic source of uncertainty relations.

\[ \hat A=\sum_n a_n P_n,\qquad P_n=|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|,\qquad P_mP_n=\delta_{mn}P_n. \] \[ p(a_n)=\langle\psi|P_n|\psi\rangle=|\langle a_n|\psi\rangle|^2,\qquad \langle A\rangle=\sum_n a_n p(a_n). \]

Hermiticity is what makes measurement results real and makes the eigenvectors usable as an orthonormal coordinate system for probabilities.

\[ |\psi\rangle=c_1|e_1\rangle+c_2|e_2\rangle,\qquad c'_i=\langle e'_i|\psi\rangle,\qquad c'=U^\dagger c. \] \[ \hat A'=U^\dagger\hat A U,\qquad \langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=c^\dagger A c=c'^\dagger A'c'. \]

Matrix mechanics is bookkeeping for this invariance. The column vector and matrix entries change with the chosen basis; probabilities and expectation values do not.

\[ I=\sum_n |e_n\rangle\langle e_n|,\qquad |\psi\rangle=I|\psi\rangle=\sum_n |e_n\rangle\langle e_n|\psi\rangle. \] \[ \sum_n |c_n|^2=1,\qquad \langle A\rangle=\sum_{m,n}c_m^*A_{mn}c_n. \]

Completeness is the bridge between abstract kets and concrete calculations. It lets us insert a convenient basis anywhere, then compute amplitudes, probabilities, and matrix elements.

\[ B(\alpha)=\cos\alpha\,\sigma_z+\sin\alpha\,\sigma_x,\qquad [\sigma_z,B(\alpha)]=2i\sin\alpha\,\sigma_y. \] \[ [A,B]=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{\(A\) and \(B\) can be diagonalized in the same orthonormal basis.} \]

The commutator is not just notation. It decides which questions can have sharp answers in the same prepared state and which sequential measurements must be treated as physically different experiments.

\[ |\psi(t)\rangle=U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle,\qquad U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar},\qquad U^\dagger U=I. \] \[ |\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|E_n\rangle,\qquad |c_n(t)|^2=|c_n(0)|^2. \]

Energy measurements are stationary in a time-independent Hamiltonian, but any observable that is not diagonal in the energy basis can oscillate because it compares components with different phases.

\[ |\psi_S(t)\rangle=U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle,\qquad A_H(t)=U^\dagger(t)A_SU(t). \] \[ \langle\psi_S(t)|A_S|\psi_S(t)\rangle =\langle\psi(0)|A_H(t)|\psi(0)\rangle. \] \[ \frac{dA_H}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,A_H]+\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}\right)_H. \]

These are not different physical theories. They are two coordinate choices for the same transition amplitudes and expectation values.

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\langle A\rangle =\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat H,\hat A]\rangle +\left\langle\frac{\partial \hat A}{\partial t}\right\rangle. \] \[ \frac{d}{dt}\langle x\rangle=\frac{\langle p\rangle}{m},\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\langle p\rangle=-\left\langle\frac{dV}{dx}\right\rangle. \] \[ \frac{d}{dt}\langle H\rangle=0 \quad \text{when } \partial \hat H/\partial t=0. \]

Conservation laws come from symmetries: if an observable commutes with \(\hat H\), its expectation is constant in time.

Deep learning checkpoint

Do not memorize the postulates as prose. Practice translating each physical question into: state, operator, basis, probability amplitude, and expectation value.

8. Solvable systems

Exact solutions teach the grammar of spectra.

The time-independent Schrodinger equation is an eigenvalue problem: \( \hat H\psi_n = E_n\psi_n \). Bound states are normalizable eigenfunctions. Their energy spectrum can be discrete, continuous, or mixed depending on the potential and boundary conditions.

\[ \int_0^a \psi_n^*(x)\psi_m(x)\,dx=\delta_{nm},\qquad \Psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}. \] \[ c_n=\int_0^a \psi_n^*(x)\Psi(x,0)\,dx,\qquad \sum_n |c_n|^2=1. \]

Orthogonality is the algebra behind spectral expansion: projecting onto one eigenfunction removes every other stationary component.

\[ \Psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)e^{i(kx-\omega(k)t)}\,dk,\qquad \omega(k)=\frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}. \] \[ \langle x\rangle=x_0+\frac{\hbar k_0}{m}t,\qquad \sigma_x(t)=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0^2}\right)^2}. \]

The center follows the classical free-particle law, while the width grows because the dispersion relation is quadratic. This is the dynamical side of the Fourier uncertainty relation.

\[ z=\frac{ka}{2},\qquad z_0=\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}},\qquad \kappa a/2=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}. \] \[ \text{even: } z\tan z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2},\qquad \text{odd: } -z\cot z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}. \]

Finite walls make the boundary condition softer: \(\psi\) and \(d\psi/dx\) remain continuous, but the tail outside the well is exponential. As \(V_0\) grows, the roots move toward the infinite-well spectrum.

\[ (\hat P\psi)(x)=\psi(-x),\qquad \hat P^2=I,\qquad [\hat H,\hat P]=0 \quad \text{if } V(x)=V(-x). \] \[ \hat P|e\rangle=+|e\rangle,\qquad \hat P|o\rangle=-|o\rangle,\qquad \langle e|\hat x|e\rangle=\langle o|\hat x|o\rangle=0. \] \[ \langle e|\hat x|o\rangle \text{ can be nonzero, so odd perturbations mix opposite parity.} \]

Parity is a shortcut for calculation: many integrals vanish before you compute them because the integrand is odd.

\[ V(x)=-\alpha\delta(x),\qquad \psi_b(x)=\sqrt{\kappa}\,e^{-\kappa |x|},\qquad E_b=-\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m},\quad \kappa=\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2}. \] \[ \psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-) =-\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\psi(0),\qquad T=\frac{k^2}{k^2+\kappa^2},\quad R=\frac{\kappa^2}{k^2+\kappa^2}. \]

The wavefunction remains continuous, but its slope jumps at the spike. This is the cleanest place to see how boundary conditions can encode a singular interaction.

\[ \Psi(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}, \qquad c_n=\int_0^a\psi_n^*(x)\Psi(x,0)\,dx. \] \[ P(E_n)=|c_n|^2,\qquad \langle H\rangle=\sum_n |c_n|^2E_n. \]

An energy eigenstate is stationary because only an overall phase changes. A superposition is different: phase differences \(e^{-i(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar}\) move probability around the well.

Infinite square well

\[ \psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}, \qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}. \]

Harmonic oscillator

\[ \hat H=\hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\right), \qquad E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right). \]

Tunneling

For a barrier with \(E\lt V_0\), the wave decays inside the classically forbidden region: \[ \psi(x)\sim e^{-\kappa x}, \qquad \kappa=\sqrt{\frac{2m(V_0-E)}{\hbar^2}}. \]

9. Harmonic oscillator

The harmonic oscillator is the algebraic engine of quantum mechanics.

Near a stable equilibrium, many potentials look quadratic: \(V(x)\approx \frac12m\omega^2x^2\). The oscillator is therefore more than a toy model; it is the local language of vibrations, fields, photons, and phonons.

Its Hamiltonian can be factored into ladder operators. Instead of solving a new differential equation for each level, one climbs the spectrum: \(\hat a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle\).

Hamiltonian and ladder operators

\[ \hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2, \qquad \hat H=\hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\right). \]

Number states

\[ \hat N|n\rangle=n|n\rangle,\qquad E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots \] The zero-point energy is forced by the commutator \([\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1\).

Position-space wavefunctions

With \(\xi=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x\), \[ \psi_n(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n n!}}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4} H_n(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/2}. \]

10. Three dimensions

Hydrogen turns symmetry into quantum numbers.

For any central potential \(V(r)\), the Schrodinger equation separates into radial and angular parts. In the Coulomb potential, the energy depends only on the principal quantum number \(n\), while \(\ell\) and \(m\) describe angular momentum and orientation.

The wavefunction has the form \(\Psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)\). The radial probability density is \(P(r)=r^2|R_{n\ell}(r)|^2\).

Central-potential separation

\[ \Psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi), \qquad \hat L^2Y_\ell^m=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_\ell^m. \]

Hydrogen spectrum

\[ E_n=-\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots \] The degeneracy reflects the Coulomb potential's extra symmetry.

\[ \hat L^2Y_\ell^m=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_\ell^m,\qquad \hat L_zY_\ell^m=\hbar mY_\ell^m. \] \[ Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)=N_{\ell m}P_\ell^{|m|}(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},\qquad m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell. \]

The \(\theta\)-dependence controls polar nodes, while \(e^{im\phi}\) winds the phase around the \(z\)-axis. These are the angular pieces that later control selection rules and partial waves.

11. Angular momentum and spin

Spin is intrinsic angular momentum with discrete projections.

Orbital angular momentum comes from rotations in space. Spin obeys the same angular-momentum algebra, but it is not a tiny classical rotation of a material object. A spin-\(\tfrac12\) state has only two possible \(S_z\) measurement results: \(m_s=+\tfrac12\) or \(m_s=-\tfrac12\).

The commutator algebra fixes the ladder structure. Once \(j\) is known, the allowed projections are \(m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j\), and the operators \(\hat J_\pm\) move between neighboring rungs.

Angular momentum algebra

\[ [\hat J_i,\hat J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\hat J_k, \qquad \hat J^2|j,m\rangle=\hbar^2j(j+1)|j,m\rangle. \]

Ladder operators

\[ \hat J_\pm|j,m\rangle =\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,|j,m\pm1\rangle. \] The ladder stops because the square-root factor becomes zero at \(m=\pm j\).

Spin-\(\tfrac12\) measurement

\[ |\chi\rangle=\cos\frac{\beta}{2}|+\rangle +e^{i\alpha}\sin\frac{\beta}{2}|-\rangle, \qquad P(+z)=\cos^2\frac{\beta}{2}. \]

\[ |1,1\rangle=|+\rangle|+\rangle,\qquad |1,-1\rangle=|-\rangle|-\rangle. \] \[ |1,0\rangle=\frac{|+\rangle|-\rangle+|-\rangle|+\rangle}{\sqrt2}. \] \[ |0,0\rangle=\frac{|+\rangle|-\rangle-|-\rangle|+\rangle}{\sqrt2}. \] \[ |\psi\rangle=\cos\gamma\,|+\rangle|-\rangle +e^{i\phi}\sin\gamma\,|-\rangle|+\rangle. \] \[ P_{1,0}=\frac{1+\sin(2\gamma)\cos\phi}{2}. \] \[ P_{0,0}=\frac{1-\sin(2\gamma)\cos\phi}{2}. \]

Clebsch-Gordan coefficients are amplitudes for changing from uncoupled labels \((m_1,m_2)\) to total labels \((j,m)\).

12. Identical particles

Exchange symmetry changes the shape of two-particle probability.

Identical quantum particles are not merely hard to label. The physical state must transform in a definite way when the particle labels are exchanged: \(\hat P_{12}\Psi=\pm\Psi\). The plus sign describes bosonic symmetry; the minus sign describes fermionic antisymmetry.

For two one-particle orbitals \(\psi_a\) and \(\psi_b\), the allowed two-particle spatial states are symmetric or antisymmetric combinations. This is the mathematical source of bunching, antibunching, and the Pauli exclusion principle.

Symmetric and antisymmetric states

\[ \Psi_\pm(x_1,x_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1\pm |S|^2)}} \left[\psi_a(x_1)\psi_b(x_2)\pm\psi_b(x_1)\psi_a(x_2)\right]. \]

Exchange eigenvalue

\[ \hat P_{12}\Psi_\pm(x_1,x_2)=\Psi_\pm(x_2,x_1)=\pm\Psi_\pm(x_1,x_2). \] Observables depend on \(|\Psi|^2\), but the sign controls interference between exchanged alternatives.

Pauli exclusion

If two fermions have the same spin state, their spatial state must be antisymmetric. Then \[ \Psi_-(x,x)=0, \] so two identical fermions cannot occupy the same complete one-particle state.

13. Beyond exact solutions

Most real problems are solved by controlled approximation.

Once the formalism is in place, the work becomes strategic: identify a small parameter, a good trial space, a symmetry, or a regime where phases simplify. Approximation methods are not shortcuts around quantum mechanics; they are how the theory becomes useful.

\[ \hat H=\hat H_0+\lambda x^4,\qquad E_n^{(0)}=n+\frac12\quad(\hbar=m=\omega=1) \] \[ E_n\approx E_n^{(0)}+\lambda\langle n^{(0)}|x^4|n^{(0)}\rangle =n+\frac12+\frac{3\lambda}{4}(2n^2+2n+1). \] \[ |n\rangle\approx |n^{(0)}\rangle+ \lambda\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{\langle m^{(0)}|x^4|n^{(0)}\rangle} {E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}|m^{(0)}\rangle. \]
\[ W_{ij}=\langle i^{(0)}|\hat H'|j^{(0)}\rangle,\qquad i,j\in\mathcal D. \] \[ \det(W-wI)=0,\qquad E_\alpha\approx E^{(0)}+\lambda w_\alpha. \] \[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}= \begin{pmatrix} -\Delta/2 & V\\ V & \Delta/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{(\Delta/2)^2+V^2}. \]
\[ \hat H'=-qEz,\qquad \langle n\ell m|z|n\ell m\rangle=0 \quad \text{for a nondegenerate parity eigenstate.} \] \[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}= \begin{pmatrix} -\Delta/2 & -dE\\ -dE & \Delta/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{(\Delta/2)^2+d^2E^2}. \] \[ \Delta=0 \Rightarrow E_\pm=\pm |dE| \quad\text{linear Stark effect},\qquad |dE|\ll\Delta \Rightarrow \Delta E\simeq-\frac{d^2E^2}{\Delta}. \]

Degeneracy changes the power of the field: linear splitting signals that the perturbation mixes states before ordinary second-order formulas are valid.

\[ \phi_\alpha(x)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha x^2/2} \] \[ E(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{4}+\frac{1}{4\alpha}+\frac{3\lambda}{4\alpha^2}, \qquad E_0\le E(\alpha). \]
\[ p(x)=\sqrt{2m(E-V(x))},\qquad \psi_{\text{WKB}}(x)\approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{p(x)}} \sin\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_1}^{x}p(x')\,dx'+\frac{\pi}{4}\right). \] \[ \int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)\,dx=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi\hbar. \] \[ \psi(x)\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}} \exp\left[-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_2}^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx'\right], \qquad \kappa(x)=\sqrt{2m(V(x)-E)}. \]

Perturbation theory

If \(\hat H=\hat H_0+\lambda\hat H'\), then the first-order energy correction is \[ E_n^{(1)}=\langle n^{(0)}|\hat H'|n^{(0)}\rangle. \] The method is strongest when nearby unperturbed levels are well separated.

Variational principle

Any normalized trial state gives an upper bound on the ground energy: \[ E_0\le \langle\phi|\hat H|\phi\rangle. \] Good physics enters through a trial family with meaningful parameters.

WKB

In slowly varying potentials, \[ \psi(x)\approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\exp\left(\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^x p(x')\,dx'\right). \] Turning points require connection formulas.

Scattering

Far from a localized potential, \[ \psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r} \] \[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2. \]

Spin and angular momentum

The algebra \[ [\hat J_i,\hat J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\hat J_k \] determines ladder operators, magnetic quantum numbers, and selection rules.

Identical particles

Exchange symmetry imposes \[ \Psi(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2)=\pm\Psi(\mathbf r_2,\mathbf r_1). \] The minus sign for fermions leads to the Pauli exclusion principle.

Problem ladder for deep study

  1. Normalize a Gaussian wave packet and compute \(\langle x\rangle\), \(\langle p\rangle\), \(\Delta x\), and \(\Delta p\).
  2. Solve the infinite square well and prove orthogonality of \(\psi_m\) and \(\psi_n\).
  3. Use ladder operators to derive the harmonic oscillator spectrum without solving a differential equation.
  4. Compute the first-order Stark shift in a simple two-level truncation.
  5. Use WKB quantization to estimate bound-state energies in a slowly varying potential.
  6. Derive a differential cross section from a given scattering amplitude.

14. Scattering

Scattering turns a potential into an angular probability map.

In a scattering experiment, a beam comes in with a well-defined momentum and the detector records how much probability is redirected into each solid angle. Far from the target, the wavefunction has an incoming plane-wave part and an outgoing spherical-wave part.

The measurable object is the differential cross section: \(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2\). In the Born approximation, \(f(\theta)\) is controlled by the Fourier transform of the potential at momentum transfer \(q=2k\sin(\theta/2)\).

Asymptotic form

\[ \psi(\mathbf r)\sim e^{ikz}+f(\theta)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}, \qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2. \]

Momentum transfer

\[ q=|\mathbf k'-\mathbf k|=2k\sin\frac{\theta}{2}. \] Small angles mean small momentum transfer; backward scattering probes shorter structure.

Born approximation

\[ f(\theta)\approx-\frac{2m}{4\pi\hbar^2} \int e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}V(\mathbf r)\,d^3r. \] The first Born approximation is strongest when the potential is weak enough that one scattering event dominates.

\[ V(r)=\frac{\kappa}{r},\qquad \eta=\frac{m\kappa}{\hbar^2 k}. \] \[ f_C(\theta)=-\frac{\eta}{2k\sin^2(\theta/2)} \exp\left[-2i\eta\ln\sin\frac{\theta}{2}+2i\sigma_0\right]. \] \[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} =\frac{\eta^2}{4k^2\sin^4(\theta/2)} =\left(\frac{\kappa}{4E}\right)^2\csc^4\frac{\theta}{2}. \]

The Coulomb phase changes the amplitude, but the measurable angular distribution matches the classical Rutherford law.

\[ f(\theta)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_\ell(\theta), \] \[ a_\ell(\theta)=(2\ell+1)e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta). \] \[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2. \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{4\pi}{k^2} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\sin^2\delta_\ell. \]

\(\ell=0\) gives isotropic \(s\)-wave scattering; higher \(\ell\) add angular structure.

\[ f(\theta)=\frac{1}{2ik}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1) \left(S_\ell-1\right)P_\ell(\cos\theta), \qquad S_\ell=\eta_\ell e^{2i\delta_\ell}. \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{4\pi}{k}\operatorname{Im} f(0). \] \[ \sigma_{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)|1-S_\ell|^2, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{abs}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)(1-\eta_\ell^2). \]

With \(\eta_\ell=1\), scattering is purely elastic; \(\eta_\ell<1\) means probability is removed into other channels, but the optical theorem still balances the flux.

15. Time-dependent transitions

A time-dependent perturbation moves probability between levels.

Stationary states do not change their measurement probabilities by themselves. Transitions happen when the Hamiltonian contains a time-dependent perturbation, such as an oscillating electric field coupling two atomic levels.

For a driven two-level system, resonance matters. If the drive frequency is close to the level spacing, probability can slosh coherently between \(|g\rangle\) and \(|e\rangle\). With detuning \(\Delta\) and coupling \(\Omega\), the excited-state probability follows the Rabi form.

Driven two-level probability

\[ P_e(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega_R^2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\Omega_R t}{2}\right), \qquad \Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}. \]

Transition amplitude

First-order time-dependent perturbation theory gives \[ c_f^{(1)}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t \langle f|\hat H'(t')|i\rangle e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt'. \] Resonant phases add coherently; off-resonant phases mostly cancel.

Fermi golden rule

For transitions into many nearby final states, \[ \Gamma_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar} |\langle f|\hat H'|i\rangle|^2\rho(E_f). \] The density of final states turns coherent amplitude growth into a transition rate.

\[ \langle n'\ell'm'|\hat{\mathbf d}\cdot\mathbf e_q|n\ell m\rangle \propto R_{n'\ell',n\ell}\int Y_{\ell'}^{m'*}Y_1^qY_\ell^m\,d\Omega. \] \[ \Delta \ell=\pm1,\qquad \Delta m=q,\qquad q=0,\pm1. \] \[ \Pi_f\Pi_i=-1 \quad \text{because } \hat{\mathbf d}=-e\mathbf r \text{ is odd under parity.} \]

A transition can be resonant and still dark if the angular integral vanishes by symmetry.

\[ E_{n,j}\simeq -\frac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^2} \left[ 1+\frac{\alpha^2}{n^2} \left(\frac{n}{j+\tfrac12}-\frac34\right) \right]. \] \[ j=\ell\pm\tfrac12,\qquad m_j=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j. \] \[ g_j=1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-\ell(\ell+1)}{2j(j+1)},\qquad \Delta E_Z=\mu_B g_j m_j B. \]

Fine structure depends on \(j\), while the weak-field Zeeman shift resolves the \(m_j\) degeneracy.

\[ \hat H_{\mathrm{hf}}=A\,\hat{\mathbf I}\cdot\hat{\mathbf J} =\frac{A}{2}\left(\hat F^2-\hat I^2-\hat J^2\right), \qquad \hat{\mathbf F}=\hat{\mathbf I}+\hat{\mathbf J}. \] \[ F=|I-J|, |I-J|+1,\ldots,I+J, \qquad E_F=\frac{A}{2}\left[F(F+1)-I(I+1)-J(J+1)\right]. \] \[ \Delta E_{F,m_F}\simeq g_F\mu_B m_F B, \qquad m_F=-F,-F+1,\ldots,F. \]

Hyperfine spectroscopy is a precision probe: the ground-state hydrogen \(F=1\to F=0\) splitting is the radio line used to map neutral hydrogen in astronomy.

\[ |\psi(0)\rangle=|n(0)\rangle \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |\psi(t)\rangle\approx e^{i\theta_n(t)}e^{i\gamma_n(t)}|n(t)\rangle. \] \[ \left|\frac{\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle}{(E_m-E_n)^2}\right|\ll\frac{1}{\hbar} \quad (m\ne n). \] \[ \gamma_n=i\int \langle n(R)|\nabla_R n(R)\rangle\cdot dR. \] \[ \gamma_\pm=\mp\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{solid}}}{2} \quad \text{for spin-\(\tfrac12\) around a closed cone.} \]

The dynamical phase depends on elapsed time and energy; the Berry phase depends only on the path in parameter space.

16. Chapter-level study guide

Each topic should end with a derivation you can reproduce.

A deep quantum mechanics course is learned by rebuilding the machinery yourself. The guide below is the current project backlog for turning this article into a full study text: every chapter has a conceptual target, a mathematical derivation target, and a problem ladder.

01 Wave mechanics and probability From \(\Psi\) to measurable distributions

Build the interpretation of \(|\Psi(x,t)|^2\), normalization, expectation values, and the continuity equation: \[ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}=0 \] \[ j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} -\Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\right). \]

  • Derive conservation of total probability from the Schrodinger equation.
  • Compute \(\langle x\rangle\), \(\langle p\rangle\), \(\Delta x\), and \(\Delta p\) for a Gaussian.
  • Explain why global phase is unobservable but relative phase is physical.
02 Stationary states and one-dimensional wells Boundary conditions create spectra

Separate variables with \(\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), solve \(\hat H\psi=E\psi\), and connect allowed energies to normalizable eigenfunctions.

  • Derive the infinite-well spectrum and prove orthonormality.
  • Match \(\psi\) and \(d\psi/dx\) across finite potential steps.
  • Compare bound states, continuum states, and resonance-like behavior.
03 Operators, Hilbert space, and uncertainty The algebra behind measurement

Translate wave mechanics into vector spaces. Observables become Hermitian operators, and incompatible observables satisfy nonzero commutators: \[ \Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \frac12|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|. \]

  • Show that Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues.
  • Derive \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\) and the Heisenberg uncertainty relation.
  • Use basis changes to compute probabilities in a non-position basis.
04 Harmonic oscillator The first algebraic solution

Factor the Hamiltonian into ladder operators: \[ \hat a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat x+\frac{i}{m\omega}\hat p\right), \qquad \hat H=\hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\right). \]

  • Prove that energy spacing is uniform and the ground energy is not zero.
  • Construct \(\psi_0(x)\) from \(\hat a\psi_0=0\).
  • Connect coherent states to classical oscillation.
05 Three dimensions and hydrogen Symmetry separates the problem

In a central potential, separation in spherical coordinates produces radial functions and spherical harmonics: \[ \Psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi). \]

  • Identify degeneracy from rotational symmetry.
  • Interpret quantum numbers \(n\), \(\ell\), and \(m\).
  • Sketch radial probability \(r^2|R_{n\ell}(r)|^2\) for low-lying states.
06 Spin, angular momentum, and addition Discrete directions from noncommuting generators

Angular momentum is governed by \[ [\hat J_i,\hat J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\hat J_k, \qquad \hat J_\pm|j,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}|j,m\pm1\rangle. \]

  • Derive allowed \(m\) values using ladder operators.
  • Represent spin-\(\tfrac12\) states with Pauli matrices.
  • Use Clebsch-Gordan logic to combine two spins.
07 Approximation methods How real systems become calculable

Perturbation theory, the variational principle, and WKB each preserve a different structure: algebraic corrections, energy bounds, and semiclassical phase.

  • Compute first-order corrections for a weak anharmonic perturbation.
  • Diagonalize a two-state perturbation matrix when unperturbed states are degenerate.
  • Use a variational Gaussian to estimate a ground-state energy.
  • Apply WKB quantization \(\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)\,dx=(n+\tfrac12)\pi\hbar\).
08 Scattering, identical particles, and transitions From bound spectra to experiments

Scattering amplitudes, exchange symmetry, and time-dependent perturbations connect the formal machinery to cross sections, atomic structure, and transition rates: \[ \Gamma_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|\langle f|\hat H'|i\rangle|^2\rho(E_f). \]

  • Relate \(f(\theta)\) to \(d\sigma/d\Omega\).
  • Compute \(\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}\) from partial-wave phase shifts.
  • Build symmetric and antisymmetric two-particle states.
  • Use selection rules to decide which transitions are allowed.
